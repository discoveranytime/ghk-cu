GHK-Cu

The copper peptide — a tiny molecule your own blood used to carry in abundance. This page walks through what it is, what it's claimed to do, what the research actually supports, and what people online report. Claims vs. evidence, side by side.

A tripeptide your body already makes, bound to copper, with levels that fall ~60% between age 20 and 60. It has the strongest human evidence of any peptide in this series — real trials on skin — but most dramatic claims (gene resets, systemic repair) come from lab studies, not people. 01 What it is

Three beads and a copper coin

GHK-Cu is one of the smallest famous peptides: just three amino acids — glycine, histidine, lysine — holding onto a single copper ion. Your body makes it, carries it in blood, saliva, and urine, and releases it when tissue is damaged. Then, quietly, your levels fall off a cliff with age.

Three amino acids, one copper ion. Small enough to slip where larger proteins can't.
The decline tracks the visible slowdown in skin repair, firmness, and wound healing with age.

How it was found · young blood, old cells

1973

At UC San Francisco, biochemist Loren Pickart incubated liver cells from older donors in blood plasma from young donors — and the old cells restarted producing protein at youthful rates. He traced the effect to a factor in plasma's albumin fraction: a tiny tripeptide with a fierce grip on copper.

1977

Harvard chemists confirmed the exact sequence — glycyl-L-histidyl-L-lysine — and follow-up work showed the copper complex is the biologically active form. Pickart proposed GHK-Cu works as a copper shuttle, ferrying copper into cells to switch on repair.

A life's work

Pickart spent the rest of his career on this one molecule — publishing extensively, founding the company Skin Biology, and championing GHK-Cu for wound healing and skin aging until his death in 2023, at age 85. Fifty years on a single tripeptide.

Plain EnglishOld cells bathed in young blood started acting young again — and the active ingredient turned out to be this three-amino-acid copper carrier. One scientist then bet his entire career that it mattered.

The molecule · GHK-Cu

  • 3 amino acids (glycine–histidine–lysine) bound to one copper(II) ion — the copper complex is the active form.
  • First isolated from human blood plasma in 1973, during research into why young plasma made old tissue behave younger.
  • Found in plasma, saliva, and urine; released from a protein called SPARC when tissue is injured.
  • Copper is not decoration — it's a working cofactor for enzymes that build collagen and fight oxidative damage.
Plain EnglishA tiny delivery truck your body already owns: three protein beads carrying one copper coin to wherever repair work is happening. The catch is the fleet shrinks ~60% between your 20s and 60s.

Why copper matters · copper does the heavy lifting

  • Copper powers lysyl oxidase, the enzyme that cross-links collagen and elastin into strong fibers.
  • It's also essential to superoxide dismutase, one of the body's frontline antioxidant enzymes.
  • GHK without copper is a much weaker signal — the complex does the work.
  • This is also why GHK-Cu products are visibly blue: that's the copper showing.
Plain EnglishCopper is the tool, the peptide is the handle. Enzymes that stitch skin together and mop up oxidative damage literally cannot run without copper — GHK-Cu is one way the body delivers it.
One bead swapped, same copper job — but the research stories diverge from here.

GHK-Cu — the natural one · glycyl-histidyl-lysine

  • Occurs naturally in human plasma — first isolated in 1973 by researcher Loren Pickart.
  • Levels decline with age (~60% from 20 to 60) — the "replenishment" story behind its use.
  • Studied for decades for tissue repair, collagen production, and skin remodeling.
  • Acts as a signaling molecule influencing healing and anti-aging genes in fibroblasts.
  • Carries the far larger evidence base: lab, animal, and some human topical studies.
Plain EnglishThe original with the track record. Your body made this one first, scientists have studied it for 50 years, and it's the better-researched pick for general skin repair.

AHK-Cu — the synthetic relative · alanyl-histidyl-lysine

  • Does not occur naturally — created by swapping the first amino acid (glycine → alanine).
  • Lab research focuses on hair follicles and the dermal papilla cells that steer hair growth.
  • Cell and tissue studies show stronger stimulation of those follicle cells than GHK-Cu, plus growth factors supporting blood-vessel formation around follicles.
  • Published research is far more limited: mainly lab and ex-vivo follicle work, essentially no robust human trials.
Plain EnglishThe specialist with the thinner file. One tweak turned a skin generalist into a hair-follicle specialist — interesting in the lab, unproven in people.

Who looks at which

Skin

Skin aging, firmness, fine lines, scar appearance — the GHK-Cu crowd, usually via topical creams or serums, where its evidence actually lives.

Hair

Thinning hair or scalp health — the AHK-Cu interest, driven by its follicle-focused lab data rather than human trials.

Lab

Regenerative biology, wound healing, copper delivery — researchers and clinicians work with both as tools, not treatments.

Plain EnglishNeither is an FDA-approved drug for these uses — they live in cosmetics or as research compounds. The use case follows the evidence: skin for GHK-Cu, hair follicles (in the lab) for AHK-Cu.
Same copper, different spotlight: GHK-Cu is the generalist with the track record; AHK-Cu is the hair-follicle specialist with the thinner file. Don't let one's evidence lend credibility to the other.
02 Why people care

From lab curiosity to skincare staple

Most peptides in this series live in forums and gray markets. GHK-Cu took a different road: through the 1980s–90s it became a legitimate cosmetic and wound-care ingredient, sold in creams and serums for over thirty years. The internet's injectable fascination is much newer — and far less studied.

The cosmetic track has decades of use; the injectable track is forum-era and essentially unstudied.

Three reasons it broke through (and one caveat)

Skin

The strongest case: human trials on photoaged skin — density, thickness, fine lines — with results comparable to established actives in some measures.

Wounds

A randomized trial in diabetic ulcers showed dramatically better closure than control — a hard clinical endpoint, not a cosmetic one.

Familiarity

It's already in your body and has sat in skincare for 30+ years, which makes it feel safer than exotic research chemicals — a feeling, not a proof.

Caveat

Almost all strong evidence is topical. The injectable/systemic use hyped online has no controlled human trials behind it.

Plain EnglishGHK-Cu earned its fame the old-fashioned way — decades in creams and a few real skin trials — then the internet took that reputation and stretched it onto injectable claims the research never made.

The road to the skincare aisle

Late 1980s

Researchers in Reims, France showed GHK-Cu stimulated collagen synthesis in skin fibroblasts at twice the rate of non-collagen proteins, increased wound protein and glycosaminoglycan accumulation, and accelerated connective-tissue healing in rat wound models. A control peptide with a scrambled sequence did nothing — confirming the effect was specific to GHK-Cu.

1990s–2000s

GHK-Cu crossed into cosmetics under the INCI name copper tripeptide-1 — anti-aging creams, serums, eye creams. Controlled human studies followed: daily facial cream for 12 weeks increased skin density and thickness and reduced sagging.

Plain EnglishThis is the part of the story that's genuinely solid. Real published human studies showing real, measured improvements in skin quality with topical use. If a copper-peptide serum is on your shelf, the science behind that specific use is legitimate.
03 The legal maze

Two routes, two legal realities

In 2023 the FDA split GHK-Cu in half: topical stayed compoundable, injectable was restricted. Then a 2026 podcast appearance promised relief "within weeks." What actually followed is messier than the headlines — and as of October 2026, injectable GHK-Cu sits in regulatory limbo.

What actually happened

Sep 2023

The FDA moved ~19 peptides to Category 2 of the 503A bulk-substances list — effectively barring compounding pharmacies from making them. GHK-Cu got a split decision: non-injectable forms landed in Category 1 (compoundable while under evaluation); injectable GHK-Cu was restricted over immunogenicity, purity, and the absence of human injectable safety data.

Sep 2024

Five peptides were pulled from Category 2 via withdrawn nominations and sent to the advisory committee (PCAC) — which voted against compounding them. None became legal. A preview of how this process can end.

Feb 27, 2026

HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. told Joe Rogan (episode 2461) that ~14 of the 19 restricted peptides would return to Category 1, with FDA action expected "within weeks." He called the 2023 ban overreach that pushed patients into the gray market it claimed to fight.

Apr 2026

The FDA removed 12 substances from Category 2 — but because nominators withdrew the nominations, not because of a safety finding, and without placing them in Category 1. Removal is not permission: the compounds landed in regulatory limbo.

May 2026

After a nominator clarified it had meant to withdraw only the injectable nomination, non-injectable GHK-Cu was restored to Category 1. Injectable GHK-Cu stayed in limbo.

Jul 2026

The PCAC reviewed seven peptides and recommended six (BPC-157, KPV, TB-500, MOTS-C, Semax, Epitalon) for the 503A bulks list. Advisory only — FDA rulemaking typically takes 6–18+ months, and nothing is compoundable until a final rule publishes.

By Feb 2027

The FDA says it will consult the PCAC on five more substances — including injectable GHK-Cu — before the end of February 2027. A related lawsuit has the FDA representing a final rule by March 2027.

Plain EnglishThe law moved; the headlines moved faster. Injectable GHK-Cu went from "barred from compounding" to "in limbo, awaiting committee review" — better than 2023, but still not legally compoundable today.

Three distinctions the excitement skips

Podcast ≠ rule

Kennedy is HHS Secretary, not FDA Commissioner. A podcast announcement — however influential — is not a Federal Register notice, and the promised "within weeks" Category 1 return never materialized as described.

Category 1 ≠ approved

Even full Category 1 status means compounding eligibility under prescription — not FDA approval, not an approved indication, not standardized dosing. What it would buy is pharmacy-grade product with COAs instead of gray-market vials.

Delisted ≠ legal

Being removed from Category 2 does not, by itself, authorize compounding. Until a substance reaches the 503A bulks list through rulemaking, pharmacies compounding it operate outside the framework.

Plain EnglishAnyone selling certainty about the timeline is selling something. The direction is toward regulated access; the date is not.
04 Proposed mechanisms

What it's supposed to do

GHK-Cu's proposed jobs split into two very different confidence levels: copper delivery and collagen support (well-grounded biochemistry) and broad gene reprogramming (a striking lab finding with debated meaning). Keep those tiers separate in your head.

Copper delivery · the grounded tier

  • Copper shuttle — GHK binds copper tightly and delivers it where enzymes need it.
  • Lysyl oxidase uses that copper to cross-link collagen and elastin into strong fibers.
  • Superoxide dismutase (SOD) uses copper as an antioxidant defense against oxidative damage.
Plain EnglishThe least controversial part of the story. Copper is a known, essential tool for enzymes that stitch skin together and defuse oxidative damage — GHK-Cu is simply a delivery service your body already runs.

Gene modulation · the debated tier

  • ~4,048 genes shifted expression in fibroblast lab studies — repair genes up, inflammatory/destructive genes down.
  • Framed by proponents as "resetting" aged gene patterns toward younger baselines.
  • The debate: gene-expression shifts in a dish don't equal rejuvenation in a person — and the loudest claims come from researchers with commercial stakes.
Plain EnglishThe most exciting — and most oversold — finding. In lab dishes, GHK-Cu nudges thousands of genes toward repair mode. That's genuinely interesting, but a dish is not a face, and "gene reset" is marketing language, not a medical one.
The honest summary: copper delivery is textbook biochemistry; the gene story is a real lab signal wrapped in heavy marketing. Neither tier is the same as proven human benefit — that's chapter 04's job.

Greedier than albumin · log K 16.44

The number

GHK's published copper binding constant is log K = 16.44 — slightly higher than 16.2 for human serum albumin, the blood's primary copper carrier and a vastly larger protein. In practical terms, GHK can pull copper directly off albumin. For a three-amino-acid peptide, that is punching absurdly above its weight.

The trick

GHK grips copper so tightly it silences copper's redox activity — free copper generates reactive oxygen species and damages proteins — while still delivering it to cellular targets. An armored escort for one of the body's most essential and most dangerous micronutrients.

The whisper

GHK-Cu shows biological activity at 10−12 molar, with maximum stimulation around 1 nanomolar — roughly 0.4 ng/mL. Most drugs need micro- to millimolar concentrations to do anything. This one works at doses a thousand to a million times lower.

Plain EnglishA three-bead peptide that out-grips the biggest copper carrier in your blood — then handcuffs copper's toxic side while keeping its useful side. And it does it all at concentrations so low most lab equipment strains to measure them.

The gene hit-list · what the 4,048 actually include

SIRT1 ↑ / STAT3 ↓

Upregulates SIRT1 — the "longevity gene" resveratrol famously failed to meaningfully activate — while suppressing phosphorylated STAT3. Cell studies, nanomolar concentrations.

NRF2 ↑ / NF-κB ↓

Activates the NRF2 antioxidant pathway and suppresses NF-κB inflammatory signaling; interferes with TGF-β1/SMAD, a master driver of fibrosis. Pulmonary fibrosis models.

Growth factors ↑

Stimulates BDNF (brain), BMP2 (bone), and VEGF (blood vessels) via the copper chaperones SOD1, CCS, and ATOX1. Lab studies.

Gut barrier ↑

Increases tight-junction proteins ZO-1 and occludin; in a mouse colitis model, reduced disease scores, suppressed TNF-α / IL-6 / IL-1β, and calmed Th17 inflammation. Animal model.

Lungs & nerves

Helped in bleomycin lung-fibrosis, silicosis, and asthma models — and asthma patients show lower plasma GHK than healthy controls, hinting at a real physiological role. Blocked copper/zinc-driven protein aggregation in CNS cells in vitro — cell culture, not people.

Anxiety — inverted

In rats, 0.5 mcg/kg reduced anxiety-like behavior — with the strongest effect at the lowest dose and weaker effects at higher doses. An inverted dose-response: unusual, intriguing, and very far from human data.

Plain EnglishThe gene data isn't one vague "rejuvenation" claim — it's dozens of specific pathways pointing the same direction: less inflammation and fibrosis, more repair signaling. The catch is unchanged: dishes and mice, not people.

The remodeling crew · demolition + inspectors

Build signals

Increases sulfated glycosaminoglycans — dermatan sulfate and heparan sulfate — the water-holding molecules that keep skin plump and resilient.

Demolition + inspectors

Upregulates MMP2 (enzymes that break down old collagen) and TIMP-1 / TIMP-2 (their regulators). Both the demolition crew and the safety inspectors get activated — the result, when balanced, is orderly matrix remodeling: out with damaged collagen, in with new.

Plain EnglishGHK-Cu doesn't just order new collagen — it hires the crew that hauls the old stuff away, plus the inspectors. When that balance tips, the demolition outruns the rebuild (see: the copper uglies, in "What people say").
05 Honest evidence check

What the research actually supports

Here's where GHK-Cu genuinely differs from most peptides in this series: real human trials exist — small ones, mostly topical, mostly on skin. The evidence has a clear shape: strong at the surface, thin everywhere else.

Skin The strongest ground

Status check — the non-negotiables

Cosmetic

Cosmetics don't require pre-approval, which is how it's sold in skincare — that is not the same as drug approval.

WADA

Not currently on WADA's Prohibited List — unlike BPC-157 and TB-500. That said, lists change; tested athletes should always verify the current list themselves.

Regulation

Two different worlds: topical cosmetics (decades of ordinary consumer use, regulated as cosmetics) vs injectable gray-market products (unregulated, unstudied in humans).

Plain EnglishThe most evidence-backed peptide in this series — and still a modest evidence base. Real skin trials, one strong wound trial, and a long cosmetic track record on one side; essentially nothing on injectable systemic use on the other.

The delivery problem · why anyone injects it at all

GHK-Cu is strongly hydrophilic — it loves water and hates crossing the lipid barrier of intact skin. Permeation studies through human skin found essentially negligible penetration without enhancement. Most of a serum sits on the surface; a fraction gets through — and because GHK-Cu works at nanomolar concentrations, that fraction is apparently enough for the effects seen in trials.

The injectable logic follows directly: bypass the skin, get full systemic bioavailability, and let the gene modulation, collagen synthesis, and anti-inflammatory effects run everywhere at once. From a pharmacology standpoint, that reasoning isn't stupid. But making sense and having evidence are two different things — and for injectable GHK-Cu, the human evidence column is still empty.

Plain EnglishRubbing it on your face is like shouting through a thick wall — a little gets through, and a little is all it needs. Injecting skips the wall entirely. Logical? Yes. Proven? Not even slightly.
06 What people say

Stories, not data

Recurring themes from forums and skincare communities, paraphrased. Every one is an unverified personal story — useful for knowing what to watch for, useless as proof.

Read these like overheard conversations, not results. No controls, no blinding, heavy survivorship bias — the people who saw nothing mostly don't post.
Unverified anecdote — the pulsing dot marks a paraphrased recurring theme from forums and social media: not verified, not evidence.

The "glow" reports

The single most common theme: skin looking brighter, firmer, "healthier" after weeks of topical use. Often described as subtle — the kind of change other people notice before you do.

Caveat: "glow" is the most placebo-prone outcome in skincare. Lighting, sleep, and routine changes all do this too.

Wrinkles softening

Long-term topical users frequently claim fine lines around eyes and forehead look shallower after 2–3 months. A few compare it favorably to retinoids without the irritation.

Caveat: this one has partial trial support (the 12-week studies), but forum timelines and magnitudes are self-reported and unverified.

Hair regrowth stories

Scattered reports of thicker-feeling hair and slower shedding, usually from scalp serums used for months. Often stacked with minoxidil, making attribution impossible.

Caveat: human hair evidence is the thinnest part of the research. These stories outrun the data by a wide margin.

Faster-healing nicks & spots

Users dabbing it on small cuts, blemishes, and post-procedure skin report quicker-looking recovery. This is the anecdote tier closest to actual trial data.

Caveat: closest to the evidence, but still uncontrolled. Small wounds heal on their own — that's the baseline people forget.

"Nothing happened"

The quiet majority: weeks of use, no visible change. These posts get fewer replies and sink fast, which is exactly why they matter to mention.

Caveat: non-responders are undercounted everywhere. Absence of a post isn't absence of non-response.

The blue tint

A practical complaint, not a benefit: GHK-Cu is blue, and users report tinted fingertips, stained pillowcases, and a faint blue cast if over-applied.

Caveat: cosmetic nuisance, not a safety signal — but a good authenticity tell. Colorless "GHK-Cu" is suspicious.

The "purge" debate

Some users report initial breakouts or rough skin that later clears — others say that's just irritation being rebranded as progress. The argument never resolves.

Caveat: "purging" is a contested concept in dermatology generally. Irritation is the simpler explanation.

Injectable converts — and doubters

The forum injectable crowd splits: some claim systemic benefits (joints, recovery), others point out there's zero human data for that route and call it expensive hope.

Caveat: this is the exact fault line from chapter 04 — topical has trials, injectable has stories. Don't let one borrow the other's credibility.

Two recurring reports worth naming

Thicker injection sites

Several injectable users report the skin at injection sites thickening over time — "harder and harder to inject." Whether that's a side effect or visible proof of collagen stimulation depends on your perspective. Unverified, obviously.

The copper uglies

Some users — mostly topical, occasionally injectable — report skin looking worse after starting: more wrinkled, more sagging, visibly older. The plausible mechanism: GHK-Cu upregulates MMP-1, the enzyme that initiates collagen fragmentation, even at its lowest tested concentration (a 2016 Scientific Reports finding). If the demolition crew outruns the rebuild crew, net collagen drops — temporarily. Most reports say it resolves within 2–4 weeks of stopping. Practitioner lore: start low, go slow, give fibroblasts time to catch up.

Plain EnglishThe same remodeling machinery that rebuilds skin can, running lopsided, tear it down faster than it rebuilds. Usually temporary, usually resolves when you stop — but it's the best honest explanation for why some people look worse before they look better.

Why the injectable exploded · four converging reasons

1 · Comfort

The GLP-1 and BPC-157 boom made the general public comfortable with injecting peptides.

2 · Pipeline

The skincare-to-biohacking pipeline is real: people who started with copper serums wanted the stronger version.

3 · Story

The mechanism story — "modulates 4,000 genes" — sounds far sexier than "supports collagen," even when describing related phenomena.

4 · Backfire

The FDA's 2023 crackdown paradoxically made it more desirable — restriction as marketing.

07 Safety & unknowns

Familiar doesn't mean fully mapped

GHK-Cu's safety story is genuinely better than most peptides discussed online — decades of topical use with a mild profile. But "used in face cream" and "injected systemically" are different planets, and only one of them has data.

Topical

Well-tolerated in trials and decades of cosmetic use. Reported issues are mild: temporary redness, occasional irritation, the blue tint. No serious safety signals in the topical literature.

Injectable

No controlled human safety data. Whatever the forums report, nobody has formally studied injected GHK-Cu's absorption, distribution, or clearance in people. This is the biggest unknown on the page.

Copper load

Theoretical, not documented: copper is essential but toxic in excess. People with copper-metabolism disorders (e.g. Wilson's disease) are the obvious caution group; for everyone else, systemic copper loading from peptides is unstudied.

Angiogenesis

Theoretical: like several repair peptides, GHK-Cu is linked to blood-vessel-growth pathways in lab studies — which is why some sources urge caution around active cancers. Unproven, but worth knowing exists as a question.

Purity

The gray-market wildcard. Cosmetic GHK-Cu is manufactured to cosmetic standards; unregulated injectable vials have no such guarantee — mislabeled or contaminated product is a documented problem across research peptides generally.

Injection reports

Mostly local and mild, per available reports: temporary redness, mild swelling, bruising, or a stinging/burning sensation at the site — usually fading within hours to a day or two — plus an occasional brief blue-green skin tint from the copper complex. Less commonly mentioned: small hard bumps (nodules), or mild systemic feelings like headache, nausea, or fatigue — not well documented in formal studies. Serious adverse events appear rare at typical research doses, but long-term safety data for repeated injections are limited, and no large controlled trials exist for this route.

Why the sting?

The burning or stinging some people feel is often linked to the copper itself or the solution's properties irritating local tissue and nerves — a chemistry explanation, not a sign something is working better. Regulatory bodies have raised safety questions about the injectable route specifically because of the copper content.

The bottom line on safety: topical GHK-Cu has one of the calmest profiles in the peptide conversation — but calm-topical does not transfer to injectable. Different route, different dose, different evidence: zero.

Reassuring tox signals · preclinical, not human

Skin cells

No cytotoxicity to keratinocytes at concentrations up to 580 micromolar — far above cosmetic or clinical use — and no upregulation of irritation biomarkers (IL-1α, IL-8, FOSL1). Copper chloride and copper acetate, by contrast, were cytotoxic and inflammatory at equivalent doses. The safety is in the complex, not the copper.

Lungs (mice)

Intraperitoneal doses from 0.2 to 20 mcg/g/day protected against bleomycin-induced fibrosis with no observable organ toxicity.

Zebrafish

Rescued copper-induced cardiotoxicity at 1 nanomolar, with no cardiovascular effects of its own — the compound protected against copper toxicity rather than causing it.

Plain EnglishIn every preclinical safety test so far, GHK-Cu looks clean — even protective. The honest asterisk: none of this is a human injecting it daily for months.

The copper math · ~0.32 mg per 2 mg dose

At a commonly discussed 2 mg dose, you'd be injecting roughly 0.32 mg of elemental copper (copper is ~16% of the complex by weight). For context, the European Food Safety Authority sets an acceptable daily copper intake of 0.07 mg/kg/day, with total safe intake up to 5 mg/day for adults. Dietary copper is tightly regulated at the gut — injection bypasses that regulation entirely.

Who should be most cautious

Anyone with Wilson's disease, Menkes syndrome, or any copper-metabolism disorder — plus undiagnosed liver dysfunction. For everyone else, systemic copper loading from peptides is simply unstudied.

Zinc balance

Some practitioners suggest zinc alongside GHK-Cu, since copper and zinc compete for absorption. Theoretical, but nutritionally reasonable.

Plain EnglishThe copper in a commonly discussed dose sits well under daily safety limits — but "discussed" isn't "studied," and injecting copper skips every regulatory checkpoint your gut provides.

The cancer question, both sides

The concern

GHK-Cu upregulates VEGF — blood-vessel growth — and tumors need blood supply. It's the same question raised for BPC-157. Theoretical, unproven, worth knowing.

The counter-data

Gene studies show 80+ cancer-suppressor genes upregulated and several cancer-promoting genes downregulated; GHK-Cu reactivated apoptosis in neuroblastoma and lymphoma cell lines; combined with ascorbic acid it suppressed sarcoma growth in mice. All preclinical.

Plain EnglishIt's not "angiogenesis equals cancer" — the actual data is more nuanced, and even points the other way in cell and mouse studies. But nuanced preclinical data is still not long-term human data, and that data doesn't exist.

No pharmacokinetics · the unmeasured basics

There are no published human pharmacokinetic data for injectable GHK-Cu: no measured half-life, no distribution data, no evidence on whether daily injections accumulate in tissue over weeks. What exists is inference — it's hydrophilic (likely extracellular-fluid distribution), binds albumin, is probably cleared renally, and histidine appears to be a key breakdown fragment. Likely and probably are not measured.

Forum protocols

Dosing schedules circulate online (daily subcutaneous, cycled on/off) — but they come from forum consensus and practitioner experimentation, not clinical guidelines. This page still won't give you one: there is no studied protocol to give.

08 Layman terms

The glossary, no PhD required

Every term gets two layers: the scientific meaning, then plain English. Tap to expand.

Scientific MeaningA peptide is a short chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, which form when the carboxyl group of one amino acid links to the amino group of the next. A tripeptide contains exactly three amino-acid residues, making it one of the smallest functional peptide units. Because of their small size, tripeptides are generally more chemically stable and better able to penetrate the skin's outer barrier than large proteins. Their size also makes them less likely to provoke an immune response. In cosmetic and pharmaceutical research, tripeptides are studied as signaling molecules that can interact with cell-surface receptors despite their minimal structure.

Plain EnglishA chain made of exactly three building blocks. Amino acids are the building blocks of all proteins, but most proteins are hundreds or thousands of blocks long, while a tripeptide is just three. Being this small makes tripeptides stable and better able to slip through the skin's surface than big proteins. Their tiny size also means the immune system is less likely to notice them. Researchers study them because even something this small can still send signals to cells.

Scientific MeaningAHK-Cu is a synthetic copper-peptide analog of GHK-Cu in which the first amino acid is alanine instead of glycine, giving alanyl-histidyl-lysine bound to a copper(II) ion. Scientific MeaningAnagen is the active growth phase of the hair cycle, during which cells in the hair follicle's bulb divide rapidly and the hair shaft lengthens. The honest summary

If you remember nothing else:

Your body makes it — then stops

GHK-Cu is endogenous, and plasma levels fall ~60% from age 20 to 60. The "replenishment" story is the most honest frame for it.

A close relative exists

AHK-Cu differs by a single amino acid and is studied mainly for hair follicles — with a far thinner evidence file than GHK-Cu.

Best-evidenced peptide in the series

Real human skin trials and one strong wound-healing RCT — modest samples, but more than BPC-157 or TB-500 can show.

Topical is the evidence; injectable is the hype

Nearly everything proven is about creams and serums. The forum injectable scene has zero controlled human trials.

Sold via the cosmetic channel. Notably absent from WADA's list — unlike its peptide cousins.

Unknowns remain

GHK-Cu

The copper peptide — a tiny molecule your own blood used to carry in abundance. This page walks through what it is, what it's claimed to do, what the research actually supports, and what people online report. Claims vs. evidence, side by side.

A tripeptide your body already makes, bound to copper, with levels that fall ~60% between age 20 and 60. 01 What it is

Three beads and a copper coin

GHK-Cu is one of the smallest famous peptides: just three amino acids — glycine, histidine, lysine — holding onto a single copper ion. Your body makes it, carries it in blood, saliva, and urine, and releases it when tissue is damaged. Then, quietly, your levels fall off a cliff with age.

Three amino acids, one copper ion. Small enough to slip where larger proteins can't.
The decline tracks the visible slowdown in skin repair, firmness, and wound healing with age.

The molecule · GHK-Cu

  • 3 amino acids (glycine–histidine–lysine) bound to one copper(II) ion — the copper complex is the active form.
  • First isolated from human blood plasma in 1973, during research into why young plasma made old tissue behave younger.
  • Found in plasma, saliva, and urine; released from a protein called SPARC when tissue is injured.
  • Copper is not decoration — it's a working cofactor for enzymes that build collagen and fight oxidative damage.
Plain EnglishA tiny delivery truck your body already owns: three protein beads carrying one copper coin to wherever repair work is happening. The catch is the fleet shrinks ~60% between your 20s and 60s.

Why copper matters · copper does the heavy lifting

  • Copper powers lysyl oxidase, the enzyme that cross-links collagen and elastin into strong fibers.
  • It's also essential to superoxide dismutase, one of the body's frontline antioxidant enzymes.
  • GHK without copper is a much weaker signal — the complex does the work.
  • This is also why GHK-Cu products are visibly blue: that's the copper showing.
Plain EnglishCopper is the tool, the peptide is the handle. Enzymes that stitch skin together and mop up oxidative damage literally cannot run without copper — GHK-Cu is one way the body delivers it.
One bead swapped, same copper job — but the research stories diverge from here.

GHK-Cu — the natural one · glycyl-histidyl-lysine

  • Occurs naturally in human plasma — first isolated in 1973 by researcher Loren Pickart.
  • Levels decline with age (~60% from 20 to 60) — the "replenishment" story behind its use.
  • Studied for decades for tissue repair, collagen production, and skin remodeling.
  • Acts as a signaling molecule influencing healing and anti-aging genes in fibroblasts.
  • Carries the far larger evidence base: lab, animal, and some human topical studies.
Plain EnglishThe original with the track record. Your body made this one first, scientists have studied it for 50 years, and it's the better-researched pick for general skin repair.

AHK-Cu — the synthetic relative · alanyl-histidyl-lysine

  • Does not occur naturally — created by swapping the first amino acid (glycine → alanine).
  • Lab research focuses on hair follicles and the dermal papilla cells that steer hair growth.
  • Cell and tissue studies show stronger stimulation of those follicle cells than GHK-Cu, plus growth factors supporting blood-vessel formation around follicles.
  • Published research is far more limited: mainly lab and ex-vivo follicle work, essentially no robust human trials.
Plain EnglishThe specialist with the thinner file. One tweak turned a skin generalist into a hair-follicle specialist — interesting in the lab, unproven in people.

Who looks at which

Skin

Skin aging, firmness, fine lines, scar appearance — the GHK-Cu crowd, usually via topical creams or serums, where its evidence actually lives.

Hair

Thinning hair or scalp health — the AHK-Cu interest, driven by its follicle-focused lab data rather than human trials.

Lab

Regenerative biology, wound healing, copper delivery — researchers and clinicians work with both as tools, not treatments.

Plain EnglishNeither is an FDA-approved drug for these uses — they live in cosmetics or as research compounds. The use case follows the evidence: skin for GHK-Cu, hair follicles (in the lab) for AHK-Cu.
Same copper, different spotlight: GHK-Cu is the generalist with the track record; AHK-Cu is the hair-follicle specialist with the thinner file. Don't let one's evidence lend credibility to the other.
02 Why people care

From lab curiosity to skincare staple

Most peptides in this series live in forums and gray markets. GHK-Cu took a different road: through the 1980s–90s it became a legitimate cosmetic and wound-care ingredient, sold in creams and serums for over thirty years. The internet's injectable fascination is much newer — and far less studied.

The cosmetic track has decades of use; the injectable track is forum-era and essentially unstudied.

Three reasons it broke through (and one caveat)

Skin

The strongest case: human trials on photoaged skin — density, thickness, fine lines — with results comparable to established actives in some measures.

Wounds

A randomized trial in diabetic ulcers showed dramatically better closure than control — a hard clinical endpoint, not a cosmetic one.

Familiarity

It's already in your body and has sat in skincare for 30+ years, which makes it feel safer than exotic research chemicals — a feeling, not a proof.

Caveat

Almost all strong evidence is topical. The injectable/systemic use hyped online has no controlled human trials behind it.

Plain EnglishGHK-Cu earned its fame the old-fashioned way — decades in creams and a few real skin trials — then the internet took that reputation and stretched it onto injectable claims the research never made.
03 Proposed mechanisms

What it's supposed to do

GHK-Cu's proposed jobs split into two very different confidence levels: copper delivery and collagen support (well-grounded biochemistry) and broad gene reprogramming (a striking lab finding with debated meaning). Keep those tiers separate in your head.

Copper delivery · the grounded tier

  • Copper shuttle — GHK binds copper tightly and delivers it where enzymes need it.
  • Lysyl oxidase uses that copper to cross-link collagen and elastin into strong fibers.
  • Superoxide dismutase (SOD) uses copper as an antioxidant defense against oxidative damage.
Plain EnglishThe least controversial part of the story. Copper is a known, essential tool for enzymes that stitch skin together and defuse oxidative damage — GHK-Cu is simply a delivery service your body already runs.

Gene modulation · the debated tier

  • ~4,048 genes shifted expression in fibroblast lab studies — repair genes up, inflammatory/destructive genes down.
  • Framed by proponents as "resetting" aged gene patterns toward younger baselines.
  • The debate: gene-expression shifts in a dish don't equal rejuvenation in a person — and the loudest claims come from researchers with commercial stakes.
Plain EnglishThe most exciting — and most oversold — finding. In lab dishes, GHK-Cu nudges thousands of genes toward repair mode. That's genuinely interesting, but a dish is not a face, and "gene reset" is marketing language, not a medical one.
The honest summary: copper delivery is textbook biochemistry; the gene story is a real lab signal wrapped in heavy marketing. Neither tier is the same as proven human benefit — that's chapter 04's job.

05 What people say

Stories, not data

Recurring themes from forums and skincare communities, paraphrased. Every one is an unverified personal story — useful for knowing what to watch for, useless as proof.

Read these like overheard conversations, not results. No controls, no blinding, heavy survivorship bias — the people who saw nothing mostly don't post.
Unverified anecdote — the pulsing dot marks a paraphrased recurring theme from forums and social media: not verified, not evidence.

The "glow" reports

The single most common theme: skin looking brighter, firmer, "healthier" after weeks of topical use. Often described as subtle — the kind of change other people notice before you do.

Caveat: "glow" is the most placebo-prone outcome in skincare. Lighting, sleep, and routine changes all do this too.

Wrinkles softening

Long-term topical users frequently claim fine lines around eyes and forehead look shallower after 2–3 months. A few compare it favorably to retinoids without the irritation.

Caveat: this one has partial trial support (the 12-week studies), but forum timelines and magnitudes are self-reported and unverified.

Hair regrowth stories

Scattered reports of thicker-feeling hair and slower shedding, usually from scalp serums used for months. Often stacked with minoxidil, making attribution impossible.

Caveat: human hair evidence is the thinnest part of the research. These stories outrun the data by a wide margin.

Faster-healing nicks & spots

Users dabbing it on small cuts, blemishes, and post-procedure skin report quicker-looking recovery. This is the anecdote tier closest to actual trial data.

Caveat: closest to the evidence, but still uncontrolled. Small wounds heal on their own — that's the baseline people forget.

"Nothing happened"

The quiet majority: weeks of use, no visible change. These posts get fewer replies and sink fast, which is exactly why they matter to mention.

Caveat: non-responders are undercounted everywhere. Absence of a post isn't absence of non-response.

The blue tint

A practical complaint, not a benefit: GHK-Cu is blue, and users report tinted fingertips, stained pillowcases, and a faint blue cast if over-applied.

Caveat: cosmetic nuisance, not a safety signal — but a good authenticity tell. Colorless "GHK-Cu" is suspicious.

The "purge" debate

Some users report initial breakouts or rough skin that later clears — others say that's just irritation being rebranded as progress. The argument never resolves.

Caveat: "purging" is a contested concept in dermatology generally. Irritation is the simpler explanation.

Injectable converts — and doubters

The forum injectable crowd splits: some claim systemic benefits (joints, recovery), others point out there's zero human data for that route and call it expensive hope.

Caveat: this is the exact fault line from chapter 04 — topical has trials, injectable has stories. Don't let one borrow the other's credibility.
06 Safety & unknowns

Familiar doesn't mean fully mapped

GHK-Cu's safety story is genuinely better than most peptides discussed online — decades of topical use with a mild profile. But "used in face cream" and "injected systemically" are different planets, and only one of them has data.

Topical

Well-tolerated in trials and decades of cosmetic use. Reported issues are mild: temporary redness, occasional irritation, the blue tint. No serious safety signals in the topical literature.

Injectable

No controlled human safety data. Whatever the forums report, nobody has formally studied injected GHK-Cu's absorption, distribution, or clearance in people. This is the biggest unknown on the page.

Copper load

Theoretical, not documented: copper is essential but toxic in excess. People with copper-metabolism disorders (e.g. Wilson's disease) are the obvious caution group; for everyone else, systemic copper loading from peptides is unstudied.

Angiogenesis

Theoretical: like several repair peptides, GHK-Cu is linked to blood-vessel-growth pathways in lab studies — which is why some sources urge caution around active cancers. Unproven, but worth knowing exists as a question.

Purity

The gray-market wildcard. Cosmetic GHK-Cu is manufactured to cosmetic standards; unregulated injectable vials have no such guarantee — mislabeled or contaminated product is a documented problem across research peptides generally.

Injection reports

Mostly local and mild, per available reports: temporary redness, mild swelling, bruising, or a stinging/burning sensation at the site — usually fading within hours to a day or two — plus an occasional brief blue-green skin tint from the copper complex. Less commonly mentioned: small hard bumps (nodules), or mild systemic feelings like headache, nausea, or fatigue — not well documented in formal studies. Serious adverse events appear rare at typical research doses, but long-term safety data for repeated injections are limited, and no large controlled trials exist for this route.

Why the sting?

The burning or stinging some people feel is often linked to the copper itself or the solution's properties irritating local tissue and nerves — a chemistry explanation, not a sign something is working better. Regulatory bodies have raised safety questions about the injectable route specifically because of the copper content.

The bottom line on safety: topical GHK-Cu has one of the calmest profiles in the peptide conversation — but calm-topical does not transfer to injectable. Different route, different dose, different evidence: zero.
07 Layman terms

The glossary, no PhD required

Every term gets two layers: the scientific meaning, then plain English. Tap to expand.

Scientific MeaningA peptide is a short chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, which form when the carboxyl group of one amino acid links to the amino group of the next. A tripeptide contains exactly three amino-acid residues, making it one of the smallest functional peptide units. Because of their small size, tripeptides are generally more chemically stable and better able to penetrate the skin's outer barrier than large proteins. Their size also makes them less likely to provoke an immune response. In cosmetic and pharmaceutical research, tripeptides are studied as signaling molecules that can interact with cell-surface receptors despite their minimal structure.

Plain EnglishA chain made of exactly three building blocks. Amino acids are the building blocks of all proteins, but most proteins are hundreds or thousands of blocks long, while a tripeptide is just three. Being this small makes tripeptides stable and better able to slip through the skin's surface than big proteins. Their tiny size also means the immune system is less likely to notice them. Researchers study them because even something this small can still send signals to cells.

Scientific MeaningAHK-Cu is a synthetic copper-peptide analog of GHK-Cu in which the first amino acid is alanine instead of glycine, giving alanyl-histidyl-lysine bound to a copper(II) ion. It does not occur naturally in the human body. Research interest has centered on hair biology: laboratory and ex-vivo studies report that it stimulates dermal papilla cells — the signaling cells at the follicle base that regulate hair growth — more strongly than GHK-Cu, and increases growth factors involved in blood-vessel formation around follicles. The published evidence base is narrow, consisting mainly of cell and tissue studies with essentially no robust human clinical trials for any indication.

Plain EnglishA lab-made cousin of GHK-Cu with one building block swapped — alanine instead of glycine — still carrying a copper ion. Your body doesn't make it naturally. Research has focused on hair: in lab and tissue studies, it stimulated the follicle's control cells more strongly than GHK-Cu and boosted growth factors that help build blood vessels around follicles. But the evidence is narrow — mostly cells and tissue samples in labs, with essentially no solid human trials for any use.

Scientific MeaningCopper is an essential trace element, meaning the body requires it in very small amounts for normal physiology. It serves as a cofactor — a non-protein helper molecule — for enzymes including lysyl oxidase, which cross-links collagen and elastin fibers; copper-zinc superoxide dismutase, which neutralizes harmful superoxide radicals; and cytochrome c oxidase, a key enzyme in cellular energy production. Copper deficiency impairs wound healing, connective-tissue strength, and antioxidant defense. However, free copper ions are chemically reactive and can generate damaging free radicals, so the body normally transports copper bound to carrier proteins or peptides. Excess copper accumulation is toxic, which is why copper balance is tightly regulated.

Plain EnglishA mineral your body needs in tiny amounts to run properly. Copper acts as a helper for important enzymes: one that stitches collagen and elastin fibers together, one that defuses harmful molecules called free radicals, and one that helps cells produce energy. Without enough copper, wounds heal poorly and connective tissue weakens. But loose copper is chemically reactive and can damage cells, so the body always carries it bound to proteins or peptides, never floating free. Too much copper is poisonous, which is why the body keeps its levels tightly controlled.

Scientific MeaningCollagen is the most abundant protein in the human body, forming rope-like triple-helix fibers that give skin, tendons, and other connective tissues their tensile strength. In skin, types I and III collagen predominate in the dermis, the thick layer beneath the surface. Fibroblasts continuously produce new collagen while enzymes called matrix metalloproteinases break old fibers down; with age, production slows while breakdown continues, producing a net loss. This net loss of organized collagen is the central structural cause of wrinkles, sagging, and thinning skin. Vitamin C and copper are both required cofactors in collagen synthesis, which is why deficiencies in either impair repair.

Plain EnglishThe most common protein in your body — and your skin's main structural support. Collagen forms strong rope-like fibers, mostly types I and III, in the thick middle layer of skin called the dermis. Skin cells constantly make new collagen while enzymes break the old stuff down, but as you age, production slows while breakdown keeps going, so you end up with less overall. That net loss is the main structural reason skin wrinkles, sags, and thins. Making collagen requires vitamin C and copper as helpers, which is one reason both nutrients matter for skin repair.

Scientific MeaningElastin is a highly elastic protein that allows tissues such as skin, arteries, and lungs to stretch and then return to their original shape. It is assembled from a precursor called tropoelastin, which is cross-linked by the copper-dependent enzyme lysyl oxidase into durable elastic fibers. Unlike collagen, elastin has an extremely slow turnover rate — the elastin laid down during development must last for decades, and damaged adult elastin is barely replaced. Chronic sun exposure degrades and disorganizes elastin fibers, a condition called solar elastosis that produces leathery, sagging skin. Because adult elastin is so difficult to rebuild, even laboratory-level signals of new elastin synthesis attract significant research attention.

Plain EnglishThe protein that lets your skin stretch and snap back, like a rubber band. Elastin is built from a raw material called tropoelastin, stitched together by an enzyme that needs copper to work. Unlike collagen, which your body keeps remaking, the elastin you grow up with is meant to last your whole life — adult bodies barely replace it once it's damaged. Years of sun exposure tangle and break these elastic fibers, which is a big reason sun-damaged skin looks leathery and saggy. Because elastin is so hard to rebuild, even early lab hints that something might help make more of it get researchers' attention.

Scientific MeaningA fibroblast is the principal cell type of the dermis, responsible for manufacturing the skin's structural components: collagen, elastin, and the gel-like ground substance (glycosaminoglycans and proteoglycans) that fills the space between fibers. Fibroblasts also secrete signaling molecules that coordinate wound healing and tissue remodeling. With age, fibroblasts become less active and less responsive, producing less collagen and more degradative enzymes — a shift that underlies much of visible skin aging. Because they are the cells that build skin structure, cultured human fibroblasts are the standard laboratory model for testing whether a compound might benefit skin. Nearly all mechanistic studies of GHK-Cu have been performed on fibroblasts in cell culture.

Plain EnglishThe main working cell in the middle layer of skin — basically the manufacturing crew. Fibroblasts make collagen, elastin, and the gel-like filler that sits between the fibers, and they also release chemical signals that organize wound healing and repair. As you age, these cells slow down and become less responsive: they make less collagen and more of the enzymes that break it down, which drives much of visible skin aging. Because they build skin's structure, scientists grow human fibroblasts in dishes as the standard test bed for "might this help skin?" Almost every lab study of how GHK-Cu works was done on fibroblasts in a dish.

Scientific MeaningGene expression refers to the process by which the information in a gene is converted into a functional product, usually a protein, and more broadly to which genes a cell has switched on or off at a given time. Every cell in the body contains the same DNA, but a skin cell and a liver cell behave differently because they express different sets of genes. Aging shifts gene-expression patterns toward chronic inflammation and slower repair. Researchers measure these patterns to see whether a treatment nudges cells toward a "younger" profile. A change in gene activity in cultured cells does not automatically mean a clinical benefit in a person.

Plain EnglishWhich genes a cell currently has switched on. Every cell in your body holds the same complete DNA instruction book, but a skin cell reads different chapters than a liver cell — that's what makes them different. As we age, cells tend to switch on more inflammation-related genes and fewer repair genes. Scientists can measure these on/off patterns to see if a treatment nudges cells back toward a younger pattern. But a change in gene activity in cells grown in a dish doesn't automatically mean the treatment will visibly help a real person.

Scientific MeaningAn antioxidant is a molecule that neutralizes reactive oxygen species (free radicals) — unstable molecules with unpaired electrons that are generated by normal metabolism and by ultraviolet radiation. Left unchecked, free radicals damage DNA, proteins, and cell membranes, contributing to aging and disease. The body produces its own antioxidant enzymes, including copper-zinc superoxide dismutase (SOD), which converts the superoxide radical into less harmful molecules, along with glutathione peroxidase. Dietary and topical antioxidants can supplement these defenses but cannot replace the body's enzyme systems. GHK-Cu is studied for antioxidant effects both by supplying copper to SOD and by increasing the expression of antioxidant-related genes in laboratory studies.

Plain EnglishA molecule that neutralizes free radicals — unstable, damaging molecules made by normal body processes and by UV sunlight. If free radicals aren't kept in check, they damage DNA, proteins, and cell walls, which contributes to aging and disease. Your body makes its own antioxidant enzymes, including one called SOD that needs copper to work, plus another called glutathione peroxidase. Antioxidants from food or skincare can support these defenses but can't replace the body's own enzyme systems. GHK-Cu is studied for antioxidant effects in two ways: by delivering copper to the SOD enzyme, and by nudging cells to switch on more of their own antioxidant defenses in lab studies.

Scientific MeaningAngiogenesis is the formation of new blood vessels from pre-existing ones, driven by growth factors such as VEGF (vascular endothelial growth factor). It is essential for wound healing, because repairing tissue requires increased delivery of oxygen and nutrients. The same process, however, can be hijacked by tumors, which stimulate angiogenesis to supply their own growth — this is why drugs that block vessel growth are used in cancer therapy. Several tissue-repair peptides, including GHK-Cu, have been linked to pro-angiogenic signaling in laboratory studies. Whether this activity is meaningful or risky in humans using GHK-Cu has not been established.

Plain EnglishThe growth of new blood vessels from existing ones, triggered by growth signals. It's essential for healing wounds, because damaged tissue needs extra oxygen and nutrients delivered to the repair site. But tumors can hijack the same process to feed themselves, which is why some cancer drugs work by blocking new vessel growth. Several repair-related peptides, including GHK-Cu, have shown vessel-growth signals in lab studies. Whether that matters — helpfully or harmfully — in people using GHK-Cu hasn't been established.

Scientific MeaningAnagen is the active growth phase of the hair cycle, during which cells in the hair follicle's bulb divide rapidly and the hair shaft lengthens. On the human scalp, anagen typically lasts two to seven years per follicle, which determines how long each hair can grow. In androgenetic alopecia (pattern hair loss), each successive cycle's anagen phase becomes shorter while the resting phase lengthens, so follicles miniaturize and produce progressively finer, shorter hairs. Extending anagen or reversing miniaturization is therefore the central goal of hair-loss research. GHK-Cu has been reported to enlarge follicles and prolong anagen in rodent and primate studies, but comparable human evidence is lacking.

Plain EnglishThe growing phase of the hair cycle — when the follicle actively produces new hair. On your scalp, this phase usually lasts two to seven years per hair, which is what determines how long your hair can get. In pattern hair loss, each new cycle's growing phase gets shorter, so follicles shrink and hairs come back thinner and shorter each time. That's why extending the growing phase is the main goal of hair-loss research. GHK-Cu has been reported to enlarge follicles and extend this phase in animal studies, but similar evidence in humans is lacking.

Scientific MeaningThe placebo effect refers to real, measurable improvements in symptoms or perceived outcomes that result from expectation, attention, and the ritual of treatment rather than from the treatment's pharmacological action. It is driven by psychological and neurobiological mechanisms, including the release of endorphins and dopamine in response to the belief that one is being helped. Skincare is particularly susceptible because outcomes are subjective, gradual, and easily influenced by concurrent changes such as better lighting, new routines, or closer self-observation. This is why controlled trials compare treatments against placebo or vehicle controls — only a difference between the groups can be attributed to the product itself. Subjective "glow" reports without such controls cannot distinguish a real effect from expectation.

Plain EnglishWhen you genuinely feel or see improvement because you expect a treatment to work — not because of anything the treatment chemically does. Expectation triggers real brain chemistry, like the release of feel-good molecules, and the ritual of a new routine adds to it. Skincare is especially prone to this because results are subjective and gradual: better lighting, a new routine, or simply looking more closely can all manufacture visible "improvement." That's why proper trials compare a product against a placebo — only a difference between the two groups counts as the product actually working. Glowing reviews without that comparison can't tell expectation apart from a real effect.

Scientific MeaningThe World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) is the international body that publishes the Prohibited List — the catalog of substances and methods banned in tested sports worldwide. Substances are added when they meet criteria including the potential to enhance performance, among other considerations. Several peptides discussed in performance and recovery contexts, such as BPC-157 and TB-500, appear on the list. GHK-Cu is not currently listed, which distinguishes it from those compounds but does not constitute any endorsement of its use. The list is revised annually, so absence from it is not a permanent status.

Plain EnglishThe international agency behind the banned-substances list used in drug-tested sports. Substances get added when they might boost performance, among other criteria. Some peptides people talk about for recovery, like BPC-157 and TB-500, are on that list. GHK-Cu is not currently on it — which sets it apart from those compounds, but "not banned" is not the same as "approved or endorsed." The list is updated every year, so its absence isn't permanent.

Scientific MeaningFDA drug approval is a multi-phase regulatory process requiring a sponsor to demonstrate, through controlled human trials, that a drug is both safe and effective for a specific medical use. The process typically takes many years and substantial investment, progressing from small safety studies through large randomized trials. Cosmetics follow a completely different pathway: they do not require pre-market approval, and their ingredients need only be safe for their intended cosmetic use without making disease-treatment claims. GHK-Cu reached consumers through the cosmetic pathway, which is legal and legitimate but represents a far lower evidence bar than drug approval. Longevity in the cosmetic market therefore indicates tolerability as a skincare ingredient, not proof of medical efficacy.

Plain EnglishGetting FDA approval as a drug means running years of controlled human trials to prove something is both safe and effective for a specific medical condition. Cosmetics go through a totally different door: they don't need pre-approval at all — the ingredients just have to be safe for cosmetic use and can't claim to treat diseases. GHK-Cu entered the market through the cosmetic door, which is perfectly legal but a much lower bar than drug approval. So its decades on skincare shelves show it's well tolerated as a cosmetic ingredient — not that it's a proven medicine.
08 Takeaways

The honest summary

If you remember nothing else:

🧬

Your body makes it — then stops

GHK-Cu is endogenous, and plasma levels fall ~60% from age 20 to 60. The "replenishment" story is the most honest frame for it.

🔗

A close relative exists

AHK-Cu differs by a single amino acid and is studied mainly for hair follicles — with a far thinner evidence file than GHK-Cu.

🔬

Best-evidenced peptide in the series

Real human skin trials and one strong wound-healing RCT — modest samples, but more than BPC-157 or TB-500 can show.

🧴

Topical is the evidence; injectable is the hype

Nearly everything proven is about creams and serums. The forum injectable scene has zero controlled human trials.

Copper delivery is textbook biochemistry; the 4,048-gene "reset" is a real lab signal wrapped in heavy marketing. Keep them separate.

Cosmetic channel, not banned either

Unknowns remain

Limits of this page

  • Trial figures are paraphrased from published summaries; sample sizes are small and methods vary between studies.

Hair claims run ahead of data

Please read this before you go

This page is for education and entertainment only. It is not medical advice, not a recommendation, and not an encouragement to obtain or use any compound. Nothing here establishes that GHK-Cu is safe or effective for any purpose in humans beyond what the cited research shows — and most of that research is small, topical, and preliminary.

GHK-Cu is not FDA-approved as a drug for any use. Its presence in cosmetics reflects cosmetic regulation, not drug-level proof. Injectable or otherwise systemic use in humans is essentially unstudied: no controlled trials establish its safety, dosing, or effects by those routes.

Anecdotes are not evidence. Personal stories from forums cannot prove cause and effect, and this page presents them only so you know what circulates — not so you'll act on them.

Copper is essential and toxic in excess. Anyone with a copper-metabolism disorder, a serious medical condition, or who is pregnant, breastfeeding, or an athlete subject to testing should not act on anything here without a qualified professional.
